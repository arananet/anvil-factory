---
name: content-briefing
description: Turn a topic or idea into a brief sharp enough that a researcher and a writer can produce the piece without guessing. Use when writing a content brief.
---

A brief is a set of decisions. Every decision left open here becomes a generic paragraph later.

**Audience and job**
- Name one reader: who they are, what they already know, and what they are trying to get done when they meet this piece. "Everyone interested in X" is not an audience.
- State the job of the piece in one sentence: after reading, the reader will know, believe or do what?

**Angle**
- State the single argument or promise in one sentence that someone could disagree with. A topic ("AI in testing") is not an angle; a claim ("review by a different agent catches what self review cannot, and here is the evidence") is.
- Say why this piece and why now: what it adds that the top existing pieces on the subject do not. If you cannot say, the angle is not sharp enough.
- Write the working headline and the one line takeaway. If they do not match, fix the angle.

**Claims and evidence**
- List the three to seven claims the piece must make, in the order the argument needs them. Lead with the conclusion, then the supports.
- For each claim, name the kind of evidence required (a number, a primary document, a worked example, a named person on the record) and mark any claim that currently has no source so the researcher starts there.
- Name the strongest objection a skeptical reader would raise and say where the piece answers it.

**Shape and channel**
- Name the channel and the format, and let them set the constraints: length, structure, hook time, aspect ratio, whether there is sound, what the first screen or first three seconds must carry.
- Outline the beats: hook, context, argument steps, proof, close. Give each a purpose and a rough share of the length.
- List the media the piece needs and what each asset must show.

**Voice and limits**
- Point to the house voice and give two or three specific voice notes for this piece (person, tone, what to avoid).
- State what is out of bounds: topics, claims, names, competitors, anything legal or brand sensitive, and the required disclosures.
- Define the call to action, exactly one.

**Before you hand off, check**
- Could two writers read this and produce the same argument? If not, tighten it.
- Is every claim either sourced or flagged for research?
- Does the brief say what success looks like in a way a reviewer can check?
